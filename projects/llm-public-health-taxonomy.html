A Risk Taxonomy and Reflection Tool for Large Language Model Adoption in Public Health


Large language models are already answering public health questions about vaccines, substance use, intimate partner violence, and more. In this domain a fluent answer can still be wrong and cause harm, and the consequences reach whole populations. We conducted focus groups with public health professionals and people with lived experience, showed them real AI answers on these topics, and built a taxonomy from what they saw.

Taxonomy overview

Harm reaches the person interacting with the tool, the relationship foundation of healthcare, the information that can reach many others, and the question of who is accountable when it goes wrong.

What contributes to risks

Distinctive LLM characteristics from traditional health info sources

People check the author, skim the source, read a list as a ranking. These norms may no longer apply for LLM interactions. These characteristics separate LLMs from traditional information sources and contribute to the identified risks.

* May not apply to every model or deployment. Fine-tuned systems and newer interaction modes can differ.

Information quality issues cannot be summarized by the commonly referred notion of "hallucination". Participants found nuanced types of low-quality output, some of which can pass an accuracy check and still cause harm.

Abstract

Recent breakthroughs in large language models (LLMs) have generated both interest and concern about their potential adoption as information sources or communication tools across different domains. In public health, where stakes are high and impacts extend across diverse populations, adopting LLMs poses unique challenges that require thorough evaluation. However, structured approaches for assessing potential risks in public health remain under-explored.

To address this gap, we conducted focus groups with public health professionals and individuals with lived experience to unpack their concerns, situated across three distinct and critical public health issues that demand high-quality information: infectious disease prevention (vaccines), chronic and well-being care (opioid use disorder), and community health and safety (intimate partner violence). We synthesize participants’ perspectives into a risk taxonomy, identifying and contextualizing the potential harms LLMs may introduce when positioned alongside traditional health communication. This taxonomy highlights four dimensions of risk to individuals, human-centered care, information ecosystems, and technology accountability.

For each dimension, we unpack specific risks and offer example reflection questions to help practitioners adopt a risk-reflexive approach. By summarizing distinctive LLM characteristics and linking them to identified risks, we discuss the need to revisit prior mental models of information behaviors and complement evaluations with external validity and domain expertise through lived experience and real-world practices. Together, this work contributes a shared vocabulary and reflection tool for people in both computing and public health to collaboratively reflect and assess risks in deciding when to employ LLM capabilities (or not) and how to mitigate harm.

Method

Topics

Three public health issues

Vaccines, opioid use disorder and intimate partner violence, chosen to span infectious disease prevention, chronic and well-being care, and community health and safety. Each demands high-quality information and each already carries misinformation, stigma or sensitivity.

Participants

Twenty people, two cohorts

Ten public health professionals (E1–E10) recruited by snowball sampling through state health departments, partner alliances, shelters and professional networks across Georgia, Indiana, California, Minnesota and Texas.

Ten members of the public (P1–P10) recruited via Prolific, screened for active information-seeking on the topic. Participants in the OUD and IPV sessions had lived experience of those issues.

Sessions

Six focus groups

Professionals and people with lived experience met separately. Sessions opened on current information practices, then introduced how LLMs generate text, then demonstrated ChatGPT (GPT-4o-mini) and Google AI Overview answering questions paraphrased from r/abusiverelationships, r/OpiatesRecovery and r/HPV.

Participants then brainstormed potential negative consequences, scaffolded by the socio-ecological model: individual, relationship, community, society. Two rounds of pilot testing preceded the study.

Participants

Experts (E1 to E10) are public health professionals, with years of professional experience in occupation listed in brackets. Public participants (P1 to P10) had actively sought information on the session topic.
